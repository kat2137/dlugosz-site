## The question

Scale AI put head cameras on factory workers to harvest their movements as
training data. Workers could not opt out. Hania started there: if a skill can be
recorded and replayed by a machine, what exactly has been transferred, and what
has been left behind?

Rather than argue the point, I built the machine. Hania is a robotic hand
learning to hold a needle and sew — the most legible test I could find for
whether embodied craft survives being turned into a dataset.

## What the hand had to do

I filmed around twenty people embroidering and analysed how each held the needle.
Every grip differed, but two mechanics were constant: opposing force between
thumb and index finger, and deformation of the fingertip around the needle to
stop it slipping. Threading depends on something a robot has no equivalent for —
the natural oil on a fingertip holding the spun fibres together.

That ruled out the obvious approach. Large exoskeleton-style arms struggle with
thin objects, and rigid fingers cannot do the isolating deformation the task
actually needs.

## What I built

Six joint systems, prototyped and rejected in turn. Collapsible joints, pulleys,
McKibben muscles, chevron pneumatic actuators, constrained ball joints, and
finally rolling-contact joints driven by motors and tendon, which gave the most
natural motion and matched hand anatomy most closely.

Each finger carries three joints driven by a single tendon actuator, so the
finger closes by conforming to whatever it meets rather than being posed joint by
joint. That is the mechanical equivalent of the fingertip deformation the needle
study identified — the hand wraps the needle instead of pinching at a fixed
angle. The thumb is actuated separately, because it supplies the opposing force
the whole grip depends on. Three further joints carry the arm, on servos rated to
40 kg.

Grip detection uses a time-of-flight sensor pulled apart from its housing —
emitter on the thumb, receiver on the index finger — so the hand registers a
needle by the interruption of the beam between fingertips rather than by vision.

## What broke

Nylon was the stiffest of the three tendon materials I tested against woven
elastic and 120 polyester thread, but it stretched under load and made finger
travel inconsistent. Steel replaced it for operation. Nylon stayed in the build
for what it is actually good at — lacing the rolling-contact joints together, where
the pleating needs to flex rather than hold a length.

The constrained ball joints were the more instructive failure. They produced
genuinely natural motion, but needed so much added stabilisation that the fingers
became heavy and hard to drive. Rolling-contact joints got most of the same range
with none of the mass.

The chevron pneumatic actuators bent convincingly on their own and could not lift
the weight of the PLA skeleton. The McKibben muscles reached only about seventy
degrees of flexion, well short of a human finger.

## Where it goes next

Motion capture into simulation, then reinforcement learning: recorded sewing
gestures multiplied in NVIDIA Isaac Lab and used to train grasp policies.

> The capture records the gesture. What it cannot record is the judgement behind it.
