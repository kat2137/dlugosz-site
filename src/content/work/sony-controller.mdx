## The problem

Sony's configurable controller is designed so that people who cannot use a
standard gamepad can still play. The brief asked what to do with it. Our answer
was to point it outward: most play on a controller is solitary and indoors, and
the same hardware that makes a game reachable can make it social.

## What we looked at

We surveyed a real site on foot before designing anything — terrain, natural
features, historic buildings, the people and animals moving through it — and
built a paper mock-up of the controller to test what interactions the place
itself suggested. Designing the interaction from the ground rather than from the
screen is what set the direction.

## What I built

The game maps a physical location as an explorable 3D environment seeded with
educational assets: plants, artefacts, buildings. Players move through the real
place and the game responds, with modes for exploring alone and for exploring
together.

My part was the controller interaction and the demo. I designed how the game's
functions map onto a configurable controller — which is a harder problem than
mapping to a standard pad, because the input layout is not fixed and the mapping
has to stay legible when the hardware is rearranged. That fed into how functions
signal themselves to the player, which is where the accessibility work actually
lives: if a control can be moved, its meaning has to be communicated by something
other than its position.

I then built the demo in Unity, animated it fully, and programmed the modes in
C#. It ran on the Sony controller as an interactive demonstration at the SIE
Challenge final presentation.
