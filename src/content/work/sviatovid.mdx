## The problem

Sviatovid is a Slavic deity who faces all four directions at once — the
symbolism is his all-seeing eyes, watching over the people who pray to him. I
wanted to build that literally: an object that looks back at whoever looks at it,
so the onlooker becomes the one being observed.

Which turns a piece of symbolism into an engineering problem. The eyes have to
find a face, track it, and keep tracking as it moves — and all of it has to fit
inside something a person can carry.

## Constraint

Everything lives in the head. Two motors, a camera, a Raspberry Pi, a battery and
the driver board, inside a housing 150.74 mm tall and just over 51 mm deep.

It also had to stay serviceable. A sealed prototype is a dead prototype the first
time a servo fails, so the camera bed mounts on magnets, the motor shelf lifts
out, and the battery charges through a USB-C port let into the shell rather than
by opening it up.

## What I built

A pan-and-tilt mechanism, borrowed from camera rigs rather than from puppetry.
Two axes: one motor for vertical movement, one for horizontal, with the eyeballs
carried on a yoke between them. I worked out the ranges from how far a human eye
actually travels before the head starts turning, then constrained the joints to
match.

The electronics are deliberately unexciting: a Raspberry Pi 4 running the vision,
a PCA9685 driver taking I²C from the Pi, and two MG90S servos on its channels.
Putting the servos behind a dedicated driver rather than straight onto the Pi
means the motors draw from their own supply, so a stall cannot brown out the
board doing the tracking.

Around the mechanism sits a printed shell, and over the shell, leather. The face
is designed in CAD and then formed by hand — every crease in the finished bag is
a panel seam in the model.

## What broke

The horizontal axis moves both eyeballs from a single motor, and the first arm I
designed for it was a curved, angled part — chosen so it could clear the camera
bed inside a head only 51 mm deep. It deformed. The load of driving two eyes
through one linkage was more than the printed material would hold along a curve,
and the arm flexed instead of transmitting the motion, which cost travel at the
end of the range and put the two eyes out of agreement with each other.

The fix was triangulation rather than a heavier part: a second arm at 45°, tying
the centre of the mechanism to the end-effector. That converts the bending load
into tension along a straight member, which the material handles. The curve
stayed, because the packaging still needed it — the reinforcement is what let it
stay.
